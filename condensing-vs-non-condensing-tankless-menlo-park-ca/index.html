Condensing vs Non-Condensing Tankless Water Heaters in Menlo Park, CA

Both types heat water on demand. The difference is in how they handle exhaust, and that changes the installation.

By the Menlo Park 24/7 Plumbing team · 2026

The difference between condensing and non-condensing tankless water heaters comes down to exhaust. A non-condensing unit sends hot flue gases out through a metal vent. A condensing unit uses a second heat exchanger to pull more heat from those gases, which cools them enough to produce liquid condensate and lets the unit vent through plastic pipe.

For Menlo Park, CA homes, the choice affects vent routing, drain access and cost. If you are planning a new heater or a switch from a tank, call (650) 860-1670 and describe the space.

What Condensate Means for Your Walls and Pipes

Condensate from a gas unit is mildly acidic. It needs a drain path, and many makers call for a neutralizer cartridge, and local rules may too so that the liquid does not harm the drain line. The neutralizer is a small canister of media that needs periodic checking and replacement.

A condensing unit in a garage or utility room needs a nearby floor drain, laundry drain or condensate pump. In a closet or an interior wall, plan the drain path before you buy.

Venting: Where Each Type Can Go

Metal vents for non-condensing units run hot and need clearances from combustibles. Plastic vents for condensing units run cooler and can be routed through a wall or a smaller opening. Intake air may also be piped from outside in a sealed-combustion setup, which keeps combustion air away from household air.

Always follow the maker's manual and local code for vent length, slope and termination. A plumber can check whether your planned wall location will work.

Gas Line and Electrical Needs

Tankless units draw a lot of gas for short periods, and many homes need a larger gas line than a tank uses. Both types also need an electrical connection for controls, a fan and sometimes a condensate pump.

If you smell gas during any work, leave the house first and call 911 and PG&E at 1-800-743-5000 from outside.

Which One Fits Which Home

Garage with a floor drain

Condensing is often straightforward, since the condensate can drain nearby.

Interior closet or finished space

Plan condensate and venting carefully, and ask the plumber to check the path.

Replacing an existing tankless unit

Matching the existing vent may be easiest, but verify the vent is compatible.

Tight budget, short hot water runs

Non-condensing may suit if the vent path already exists.

High use, long-term plan

Condensing's lower gas use adds up over years.

Installation Basics

A plumber sets the unit on the wall, connects water and gas, installs the vent and condensate drain, wires the controls and tests combustion and temperature. Isolation valves are added so the unit can be flushed later, as covered in our guide to cleaning tankless heaters.

Our tankless water heater page lists what we install and service, and water heater installation covers new locations and fuel changes.

Running Costs Over the Life of the Unit

A condensing unit costs more to buy and install but uses less gas for the same hot water. The savings depend on how much hot water you use. A household that runs several showers a day, laundry and a dishwasher will see the difference sooner than a couple who use little hot water.

Work out your own payback by asking for the efficiency rating of each model and your current gas use. A plumber can walk through the numbers, and a simple estimate is better than a promise.

Noise, Space and Clearances

  • Both types have a fan, so ask about noise if the unit sits next to a bedroom wall.
  • Condensing models often need a clear spot below for the neutralizer.
  • Maintain the clearances in the manual from walls, floor and combustibles.
  • Leave room to reach the service ports and the inlet filter.
  • Plan for a power outlet within reach of the unit.

Maintenance Differences

Condensing units add a neutralizer and a condensate drain to the maintenance list. The neutralizer media can be exhausted over time and needs replacing on the maker's schedule. A clogged condensate line can shut the unit down.

Non-condensing units skip those steps, but their metal vents run hotter and need inspection for corrosion and gaps. Both types need regular flushing, as in our guide to cleaning a tankless heater.
